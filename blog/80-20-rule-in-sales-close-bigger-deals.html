The 80/20 Rule in Sales: How I Went From Small Deals to Big Ones

Karthik Onteddu · October 2026 · 5 min read

Every new salesperson hits the same wall. You are working hard. Your calendar is full. You are closing deals. And yet something feels off, because the people around you seem to do less and earn more.

I hit that wall in my first five months in on-field sales. Here is what was wrong, and the one idea that fixed it.

Months 1 to 5: busy, but not big

I was new to on-field sales and new to the industry. I threw myself into learning, and I still am. The L&D training at Hilti India set me up well, with strong product knowledge, industry insight and a solid sales framework. I felt prepared.

But I noticed a pattern I could not ignore: I was making sales, but not the big-ticket ones.

To put it in perspective, I was closing 10 deals of $100, while my experienced teammates were closing 2 deals of $500. Both piles added up to $1,000. But the energy, the travel, the follow-ups and the time behind each pile were nowhere close to equal.

I was working hard, not smart. And the worst part is that it did not feel like a problem from the inside. Ten closed deals feel like progress. It took me a while to see that I was being rewarded for motion, not for impact.

The turning point

I decided to stop guessing. I did two uncomfortable things.

  • I asked my experienced teammates how they spent their days and how they chose where to put their effort.
  • I opened up to my manager about my struggles, plainly, without dressing it up.

That honest conversation is where I realized I had been completely blind to the 80/20 rule in sales.

What is the 80/20 rule?

The 80/20 rule, also called the Pareto principle, comes from an Italian economist named Vilfredo Pareto. He noticed that roughly 80% of the land in Italy was owned by 20% of the people. The same lopsided pattern keeps showing up everywhere.

In plain terms: about 80% of your results come from about 20% of your efforts. The exact numbers are never perfect, but the shape is. A small slice of what you do produces most of what you get.

In sales, that might mean a handful of accounts produce most of your revenue, or a few types of conversations produce most of your big deals. The flip side is the painful part: the other 80% of your effort is producing only a fifth of your results.

I was spreading my energy across tasks that did not move the needle. I was treating every customer, every call and every task as equally important. They are not.

How to apply 80/20 to your sales day

I audited my own days and made a conscious shift. Here is the practical version, so you can do the same.

  1. Track your time for one to two weeks. Write down what you actually do, in blocks. Calls, site visits, quotes, admin, follow-ups, travel, internal meetings. Be honest. Memory flatters you; a log does not.
  2. Connect activities to outcomes. For each closed deal, ask which activities truly pushed it forward. Which meeting changed the customer's mind? Which conversation unlocked the budget?
  3. Find your top 20%. These are the few activities and accounts that show up behind your biggest wins. For me it was deeper conversations with fewer, higher-potential customers.
  4. Find your bottom 80%. Look for tasks that eat hours but rarely show up behind a win. Chasing tiny orders, over-polishing quotes for low-intent leads, and busywork that only feels productive.
  5. Eliminate, batch or delegate the low-value work. Cut what you can. Batch what you cannot cut into one window of the day. Ask for help on the rest.
  6. Protect your best hours for high-impact work. Put the big-ticket activities in your sharpest time slots, before the small stuff fills the day.
  7. Review every week. The 20% can shift. Re-run the audit so you are always aimed at what is working now.

Notice that none of this asks you to work more. It asks you to choose better. I focused on fewer, high-impact tasks, and everything else had to earn its place.

The result: the domino effect

Within a month, I closed the biggest deal of my career so far.

But that was not the real win.

The real win was what happened next. One big deal does something a pile of small ones never does: it changes how you walk into the next room. You know what a large deal looks like from the inside. You recognize the signals sooner. You ask sharper questions, and you stop being nervous about the bigger numbers.

Each interaction felt sharper. Confidence fed better conversations, better conversations fed more big deals, and soon big-ticket sales started to become my new normal. That is the domino effect. The first domino is the hardest to tip, and it only falls when you aim your effort at the right place.

It also changed the way I think about walking away. Once you stop treating every deal as precious, you can be selective, and I wrote about that in when to walk away from a sales deal. And as deals get bigger, more people get involved in the decision, which is why how to sell to multiple stakeholders becomes a skill worth building early.

Key takeaways

  • Ask for feedback early. Growth needs honesty, not ego.
  • Stop being busy. Focus on what drives results, not on what fills the calendar.
  • Audit before you optimize. Track your time, link it to outcomes, and find your real top 20%.
  • Expect momentum. One big win builds the confidence and sharpness that make the next one easier.
  • Run your own race. I was five months in, comparing myself to people with five-plus years of experience. Perspective matters.

If you are new to sales and feel like you are working twice as hard for half the result, you are not behind. You are probably just aimed at the wrong 80%.
