Sales to Strategy: 6 Big Shifts When You Change Roles

Karthik Onteddu · October 2026 · 6 min read

Closing deals and crafting direction look like neighbours on the org chart. They are not. The first time I sat in a strategy room after years in sales, I realized I had been training one set of muscles and was now being asked to use a completely different set.

I moved from Account Management to Strategic Marketing during my rotations in the Hilti Group Outperformer program, a program that moves high-potential employees through different functions so they build a view of the whole business instead of one corner of it. It is not just about execution anymore. Here are the six shifts I felt most, with what I would tell anyone making the same move.

1. Pace vs patience

The contrast: Sales is fast, target-driven and full of adrenaline. Strategy works on long-term outcomes with no immediate scoreboard.

In sales, I knew by Friday whether the week had worked. In strategy, I could spend weeks on work whose payoff would only show up in quarters. The quiet was the hardest part, because nobody was clapping and nothing was closing.

Takeaway: Build your own scoreboard early. Set milestones you can tick off (a hypothesis validated, a stakeholder aligned, a draft reviewed) so the long game still gives you a sense of progress.

2. Certainty vs ambiguity

The contrast: In sales, success was measurable: dashboards, numbers, closures. In strategy, I had to build the scale first, then define how to measure it.

Nobody handed me a target. The first job was deciding what "good" even looked like, and that meant being comfortable with a blank page and an unclear brief.

Takeaway: Stop waiting for clarity. Write down your own definition of success, share it, and let people correct it. A wrong draft that people can react to beats a perfect plan that never leaves your head.

3. Customer-facing vs multi-stakeholder management

The contrast: Sales meant solving problems directly with customers. Strategy widened my scope to internal and external stakeholders, with diverse interests to align on-field and off-field.

Suddenly the people I had to convince included colleagues in other business units, leadership, and the customer, all wanting different things. If you want to go deeper on this skill, I wrote about it in how to sell to multiple stakeholders, and the same logic about focus applies in the 80/20 rule in sales.

Takeaway: Map every stakeholder on day one: what each one wants, what each one fears, and who really decides. Strategy is mostly alignment work disguised as analysis.

4. Data-driven vs data-creating

The contrast: With an engineering background, I have always leaned on data. Strategy taught me that sometimes you start with none.

When the numbers did not exist, I went to the field. I spoke to customers across different locations, built the data myself, and then validated it before putting it in front of anyone. The dataset became my own work product, not something I received.

Takeaway: Treat missing data as a research task, not a blocker. Talk to ten real customers, log what you hear in a consistent format, then test it against a second source before you trust it.

5. Specialist vs generalist thinking

The contrast: In sales, I focused on one trade. In strategy, I had to wear multiple hats across domains and business units.

I designed the market-requirement strategy for one of our most complex customers in India, which meant thinking about product, channel, pricing and field execution together instead of one at a time.

Takeaway: Keep your depth, but learn the vocabulary of every neighbouring function. Your sales instinct is an asset because you know what actually happens at the customer's site. Just pair it with enough breadth to connect the dots.

6. Execution vs storytelling

The contrast: Sales rewards doing. Strategy rewards the person who can make a decision easy for someone else.

This was my biggest learning. I once turned a 70-slide, in-depth strategy presentation into 7 slides for the executive team. Those 63 slides were not wasted. They were the foundation. But executives do not need the foundation, they need the decision.

What helped me cut it down:

  • Lead with the answer. Open with your recommendation and the ask, then support it. Do not build up to a reveal.
  • One idea per slide. If a slide needs two headlines, it is two slides, or one of them is not needed.
  • Move the evidence to the appendix. The 70 slides still exist, just not in the room. Be ready to open a backup slide when someone asks.
  • Write the headline as a sentence. "Market share grows 4 points if we shift focus to segment B" beats "Segment analysis".
  • Know the one question they will ask. Every executive audience has one. Answer it before they do.

Takeaway: Compression is not simplification, it is judgment. Deciding what to leave out is the skill.

The constant: customer first

One thing stayed the same in both roles: the customer-first approach. Whether it is sales or strategy, it is the end customer you are trying to solve a problem for, to make things more efficient, more seamless, and to create more value. The scoreboard changes. The reason you are in the room does not.

Should you make the switch?

An honest read, not a pitch.

You will likely thrive in strategy if you are comfortable without a daily scoreboard, you enjoy building the frame before filling it in, you like connecting people and ideas across teams, and you get energy from synthesis and storytelling.

You will likely thrive in sales if you want fast feedback, direct ownership of a number, and the thrill of a close. Neither path is better. They suit different temperaments, and many people are strongest after doing both.

If you are unsure, try a rotation, a cross-functional project or a stretch assignment before committing. That is exactly how I found out, and I came back to every customer conversation sharper for it.
