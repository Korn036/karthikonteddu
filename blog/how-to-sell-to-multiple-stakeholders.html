How to Sell to Multiple Stakeholders: Reading the Room

Karthik Onteddu · 15 October 2026 · 5 min read

You walk into a room. Five people. Five agendas. One pitch. What do you do?

Most salespeople reach for the same move: deliver the pitch they prepared, to the room as a whole, and hope it lands. It rarely does. One of the most overlooked skills in sales is reading the room, not just the people in it.

Why the room is never one buyer

In B2B, almost nobody buys alone. Purchases of any size run through buying committees, and decisions are made by consensus. Each person at the table has a different role, a different agenda, a different vocabulary, and a different definition of success. Any one of them can slow a deal, and sometimes any one of them can kill it.

That changes the job. You are not pitching a product. You are understanding psychological drivers, unspoken cues, subtle power dynamics, and what each person actually wants from this decision.

One solution, five lenses

Here is a room I have walked into many times. The solution is the same for everyone. What each person needs to hear is not.

  • The CEO wants growth and operational clarity. Talk outcomes: how this scales the business, removes friction, and gives visibility. Skip the spec sheet. Keep it short and strategic.
  • The purchase manager is focused on cost. Talk total cost, not just unit price: lifecycle, payment terms, delivery reliability, and what a cheaper alternative quietly costs later. Come ready to defend your number.
  • The design lead cares about critical specs. Talk tolerances, performance, compatibility, and proof. This person will test you on detail, so precision builds trust faster than enthusiasm.
  • The accountant watches cash flow. Talk payback period, payment schedule, and predictability. Make it easy for them to see where the money goes and when it comes back.
  • The safety head will not bend on compliance. Talk certifications, standards, and documented track record. Never approximate here. One vague answer on safety can end the conversation.

None of these people is wrong. Each is protecting something real. Your job is to show that your solution protects it too.

How to read the room

People do not always say what they mean. But if you are listening carefully, they always show you what matters. A few things I watch for:

  • Who defers to whom. Watch where eyes go when a hard question lands. Watch who gets interrupted and who does the interrupting. Titles on the invite tell you the org chart. Behavior in the room tells you the real power map.
  • The quiet one. The person who stays silent is often the real decision maker, or the person whose opinion the decision maker trusts most. Do not mistake silence for disinterest. Find a way to bring them in without putting them on the spot.
  • Shifts in tone and posture. A leaned-in listener has found something relevant. Crossed arms, a glance at the phone, or a sudden shorter answer means you have drifted off their concern. Pivot, do not push on.
  • The question behind the question. "What is the warranty?" from the accountant is rarely about the warranty. It is about future cost risk. Answer the worry, not just the words.

This is applied psychology in real time. You will not catch everything, but the habit of looking up from your slides is what separates a presenter from a seller.

The language you speak is part of the pitch

Sometimes adapting means switching between Hindi, English and Telugu in the same room. The CEO may be most comfortable in English, the plant team in Hindi, and the founder's brother in Telugu. Moving between them is not a party trick. It tells each person that you came to meet them where they are, not to make them meet you.

The same applies beyond language: formality, pace, humor, even how much small talk comes before business. Cultural adaptability is a form of respect, and people buy from those who respect them.

Practical tips that work

  • Prepare a one-pager per lens. One page for the CEO on outcomes, one for purchase on cost, one for design on specs, one for finance on cash flow, one for safety on compliance. You may not hand them all out, but writing them forces you to think in each person's language.
  • Lead with the concern of the most skeptical person. It is tempting to open with your strongest card. Instead, address the biggest objection first. Once the toughest person in the room feels heard, the rest of the room relaxes, and everyone else sees you are not hiding anything.
  • Acknowledge each person's priority explicitly. Say it out loud: "Safety is non-negotiable here, so let me show you the certification first." "I know cash flow is the question for finance, so here is the payment structure." When people feel seen, they stop defending and start evaluating.
  • Ask before you assume. A simple "What would make this a clear yes for you?" to each person turns a pitch into a conversation and hands you the map.
  • Debrief after. Right after the meeting, write down who said what, who stayed quiet, and who looked at whom. That note is gold for the next round.

Before you get in the room, and when to leave it

Reading the room assumes you are already in it. Getting there is its own skill, and I wrote about it in how to reach the decision maker in B2B sales. And sometimes, after you have read the room well, the honest read is that this deal is not for you. That is a skill too, covered in when to walk away from a sales deal.

Adaptability in sales is more than agility. It is emotional intelligence. And it is often the difference between a pitch and a closed deal.
